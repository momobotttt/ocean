**Course:** FINT 508 Human Resource Analytics for FinTech Management (HKSYU)
**Your role:** guest speaker · 4 sessions × 120 minutes · Sundays in November
**Course instructor:** Dr LEE, Tin Yun Philip — **owns the technology content**

If the block turns out to be three sessions instead, Section 12 says exactly how to merge. If it is two, fall back to the combination guide in the Guest Speaker Pack.

## 3. Teaching 13 international students on a Sunday

This section is the highest-value part of this document. A 13-person international seminar is a completely different room from a 40-person lecture, and most of what makes it work is set up before you say anything.

### 3.1 Room setup — ask for this specifically
Request a **U-shape, or one big table cluster**. With 13 people you can all sit around a table, and it changes the quality of discussion more than any activity design. Rows of desks facing forward will give you a lecture whether you want one or not.

Also ask for: a whiteboard you can leave written on all session, and confirmation that the projector takes your laptop's connector.

### 3.2 Names — learn all 13 on Sunday 1
With 13 students this is achievable and it transforms the other three sessions.
- Name cards folded on the table, session 1 and 2. Ask them to write the name they want to be called, not the one on the register.
- Use a name every time you respond to someone.
- By Sunday 2 you should be able to say *"Priya, you made the opposite argument last week — has that changed?"* That single sentence is worth more than any slide.

### 3.3 The small-class rebalance
Everything is faster with 13, so the time goes somewhere else:

| | 40-person class | Your 13-person class |
|---|---|---|
| Groups | 8 groups of 4–5 | **3 groups of 4–5** |
| Presenting | 2 of 8 groups present | **All 3 present, every time** |
| Presentation time | 24 min for a sample | **9–12 min for everyone** |
| The freed time | — | **Goes into discussion and debrief** |
| Whole-class discussion | Impractical | **Viable — and your best tool** |
| Anyone hiding | Easy | Impossible — which cuts both ways (see 3.5) |

### 3.4 International classroom — practical rules

| Do | Why |
|---|---|
| **Slow down. Consciously.** | Non-native listeners need processing time. Speed is the single biggest barrier, ahead of vocabulary. |
| **Write every key term on the board** and leave it there | Most people read English better than they hear it. A visible word list rescues anyone who lost a term ten minutes ago. |
| **Avoid idioms, sports metaphors and HK-only references** | "Move the needle", "out of the ballpark", "MPF", "Form 5" — either drop them or explain them once and reuse them deliberately. |
| **Written first, then spoken** | "Take 90 seconds and write one sentence" before any discussion. It doubles participation, and it disproportionately helps people who are composing in a second language. |
| **Structured turn-taking, not "anyone?"** | In several cultures, volunteering an answer in front of a teacher is not the norm. Going round the table, or nominating by name after a pair discussion, is not rude — it's inclusive. |
| **Repeat and reformulate answers** | "So you're saying X — is that right?" gives the speaker a correction chance and the room a second hearing. |
| **Never make anyone the spokesperson for their country** | Ask *"in a market you know well…"* not *"how do they do it in India?"* Invite, don't appoint. |

### 3.5 The introvert problem in a small room
With 13 people there is nowhere to hide, and for a quiet student that is stressful, not motivating. Protect them:
- Always **think → pair → share**. Nobody speaks to the room without having said it once to one person first.
- Let people contribute in writing sometimes — a shared doc, a sticky note, a group's flip chart.
- Rotate who reports back for the group, and say at the start that you'll do that, so nobody is ambushed.

### 3.6 Turn the diversity into content — this is your unfair advantage
Your four topics are exactly the four where national practice diverges most. Build one comparison moment into each session:

| Sunday | The comparison question |
|---|---|
| 1 Resourcing | *"In a market you know well — how long does it take to hire a specialist, and can you fire someone easily if the plan changes?"* (notice periods and employment protection change the whole planning problem) |
| 2 Recruitment | *"What is a normal job application in a market you know? Photo on the CV? Age? Marital status? What's illegal to ask?"* |
| 3 Compensation | *"Is pay something people discuss openly where you're from? Is there a legal right to know what colleagues earn?"* |
| 4 Turnover | *"Is it normal to change jobs every two years, or does that look bad on your record?"* |

Ten minutes each, and it does four jobs at once: participation, real content, genuine inclusion, and it lets you cite Llanos & Bin Ahmad (2016) on compensation and commitment differing between Mexican and Malaysian bankers with a room that has just proved the point themselves.

### 3.7 Sunday energy
Two hours on a Sunday is a real constraint. Assume attention drops after 20 minutes of anything.
- **Never talk for more than 20 minutes** without changing the state of the room.
- **Five blocks, not two.** Every run sheet below does this.
- Break at roughly the 45–55 minute mark, ten minutes, with the return time on screen as a clock time.
- If the room goes flat, stand them up: *"Find someone you haven't spoken to today, 90 seconds, tell them the one thing you'd do differently."*

## 4. The four-analytics ladder — the 12-minute version (Sunday 1)

Light, as agreed. Not a lecture on analytics. A spine.

**Put these four words on the whiteboard before anyone arrives and leave them there for all four Sundays:**

```
   DESCRIBE  →  DIAGNOSE  →  PREDICT  →  PRESCRIBE
```

**How to teach it in 12 minutes, using one HR example the whole way up:**

| Rung | The question | The same example, climbing |
|---|---|---|
| **Describe** | What happened? | Attrition was 24% last year. It cost roughly HK$11m. |
| **Diagnose** | Why did it happen? | It wasn't spread evenly — it concentrated in engineers with 12–24 months' tenure, under three particular managers, and spiked two months after the bonus was paid. |
| **Predict** | What will happen? | These 40 people are at elevated risk in the next six months. |
| **Prescribe** | What should we do? | Given a HK$2m budget and an 8-week hiring pipeline, here is where to spend it, and here is what we expect back. |

**The three sentences that make it stick:**
1. *"As you climb, the value goes up — and so does the ethical weight. Nobody is upset by a headcount number. People are upset by a list with their name on it."*
2. *"Most organisations live on rung one and think they're doing analytics."*
3. *"Every one of my four sessions climbs this same staircase. In four weeks you'll be able to place any HR question on it in about five seconds."*

**Then check it immediately** (this is the 12th minute): *"Give me any HR question — anything — and the room will tell me which rung it sits on."* Take three. Write them on the board under the right word.

**In Sundays 2, 3 and 4:** 90 seconds at the top. *"Where are we on the staircase today?"* Point at the board. That's all it needs.

## 5. Sunday 1 — Strategic Resourcing
**"How many people do we need, and can we actually get them?"**

### The one big idea
> **A headcount plan is not a hiring plan. Capacity, lead time and attrition decide what is actually possible.**

### Run sheet (120 min)

| Time | Block | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 0–10 | **Welcome + who you are** | Name cards out. Your 60-second why-me. Then straight to a show of hands: *"Hands up if you've ever waited more than three months for a job offer or a hire to come through."* Now they've participated in minute three. |
| 10–22 | **The ladder** | Section 4. Twelve minutes, then the check question. |
| 22–42 | **Input A — from headcount to capability** | Workforce segmentation (critical / core / supporting / outsourceable); VRIO applied to a FinTech firm; why the scarce thing in FinTech is a *combination* of skills, not a skill |
| 42–52 | **International comparison** | Section 3.6, question 1. Round the table — 45 seconds each, everyone speaks once. Capture the range of notice periods and firing difficulty on the board. |
| 52–62 | **Break** | Return time on screen as a clock time |
| 62–80 | **Input B — the forecast** | Opening headcount − predicted attrition + internal mobility + external hires, against demand. Time-to-fill as a hard constraint. Build / buy / borrow / bot compared on **cost and lead time together**. |
| 80–105 | **Activity — Scale to 500** | Three groups, laptops, shared spreadsheet (below) |
| 105–117 | **All three groups present** | 3 minutes each, then the room stress-tests one assumption per group |
| 117–120 | **Close** | What breaks these plans in practice. Preview Sunday 2: *"Your plan says 60 hires. Next week, we find them."* |

### The activity — "Scale Kestrel Pay to 500"
**Setup:** 3 groups of 4–5 · 25 minutes · laptops on

Give each group a link to a **pre-built shared spreadsheet** with one tab per group — role families, current headcount, an 18-month roadmap, and assumption cells (attrition %, time-to-fill by role, internal mobility %) already wired to the arithmetic. They change assumptions, not formulas. The learning is in the argument about the assumptions, not the maths.

**Each group delivers:** demand by role family → net of attrition → the two biggest capability gaps → build/buy/borrow/bot for each with cost *and* lead time → **the one assumption that, if wrong, breaks the plan**.

**Why the shared spreadsheet:** you can watch all three groups' numbers change live on your own screen while you walk around, and you can put all three side by side on the projector for the debrief. With 13 students this works beautifully; with 40 it wouldn't.

**AT4 hook** *(confirm with Dr Lee)*: this plan is a ready-made component of a group project section on strategic resourcing.

### What to prepare
Shared spreadsheet with three tabs · Kestrel Pay one-pager (profile + roadmap) · your 60-second intro · the four ladder words on the board

## 6. Sunday 2 — Recruitment
**"How do we choose, and how do we know we chose well?"**

### The one big idea
> **The funnel shows where you're losing people. Validity shows whether your choices are any good. Fairness shows whether you're allowed to keep making them.**

### Run sheet (120 min)

| Time | Block | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 0–8 | **Callback + ladder** | *"Last week your plans needed 60 hires. Today: where do they come from, and how do we pick?"* 90 seconds on the staircase. |
| 8–28 | **Input A — the funnel as a measured system** | Stages; conversion by source and stage; source *yield* vs source volume; quality-of-hire and time-to-productivity as the only two that matter |
| 28–45 | **Live research — real job ads** | Each student finds one real FinTech job ad, ideally from a market they know (below) |
| 45–55 | **Break** | — |
| 55–75 | **Input B — does selection actually work?** | Validity of selection methods — **this is your HRM home ground, teach it properly**. Then predictive scoring from work history, and prescriptive assignment, at concept level only. |
| 75–100 | **Activity — the fairness audit** | Three groups, pre-computed funnel data, four-fifths rule |
| 100–115 | **Whole-class discussion — should the algorithm screen?** | With 13 people, run this as a genuine round-table, not a fishbowl. Everyone speaks. |
| 115–120 | **Close** | Where firms actually land. Preview Sunday 3: *"You've hired them. Now what do you pay them?"* |

### Activity 1 — real job ads (17 min, laptops)
*"Find me one real job ad for a FinTech role — ideally posted in a market you know well. Five minutes. Then tell us three things: what they're screening on, what they're asking that they probably shouldn't, and what they've left out that actually predicts performance."*

Round the table, 45 seconds each. Capture on the board in three columns.

**Why this is the right activity for your class:** it uses the laptops, uses their nationalities as content rather than decoration, produces genuinely unpredictable material, and puts every one of the 13 on record having contributed. Expect the photo-on-CV, age, marital status and nationality differences to surface on their own — you don't need to prompt them.

### Activity 2 — the fairness audit (25 min)
Hand out a **pre-computed** funnel table: applications by source and stage, plus selection rates split by a protected-attribute proxy.

Groups compute two impact ratios by hand (one division each), apply the four-fifths rule, and answer: is there adverse impact, **at which stage**, and what would you change on Monday?

**The three minutes that make the session:** when a group proposes fixing it by removing the attribute from the process, show them that university, career gap and location reproduce it. Nobody forgets this.

**Pass condition:** a correct impact ratio *and* a stated limitation of using a proxy.

**AT4 hook** *(confirm with Dr Lee)*: the audit method transfers directly to a project section on recruitment analytics.

### What to prepare
Pre-computed funnel handout · four-fifths worksheet (one page) · three columns ready on the board · your validity slide

## 7. Sunday 3 — Compensation & Benefits
**"What is a person worth, and what does pay actually buy?"**

### The one big idea
> **Pay fixes pay problems. Most retention problems are not pay problems — and the way you find out is by evaluating the intervention.**

### Run sheet (120 min)

| Time | Block | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 0–8 | **Callback + ladder** | *"You planned. You hired. Now the offer."* |
| 8–30 | **Input A — reward architecture in FinTech** | Total rewards; job architecture, levelling, bands, compa-ratio; equity and vesting as *a promise about a future valuation*; the bonus-cycle attrition spike |
| 30–45 | **International comparison** | Section 3.6, question 3 — pay transparency and pay-talk norms. This one produces the sharpest differences of the four; give it the full 15 minutes. |
| 45–55 | **Break** | — |
| 55–72 | **Input B — pay equity in the data** | Compa-ratio distributions; **raw gap vs unexplained gap**; pay compression from buying in new hires above tenured staff |
| 72–100 | **The case — "Did the HK$3m work?"** | The best 30 minutes in the whole series (below) |
| 100–115 | **All three groups pitch** | What would you have set up *before* spending the money |
| 115–120 | **Close** | Reward is a system, not a lever. Preview Sunday 4: *"Next week, they leave anyway."* |

### The case — "Did the HK$3m work?"
**Scenario:** Kestrel Pay spends HK$3m on an off-cycle retention adjustment for 60 engineers. Six months later, attrition in that group falls from 21% to 13%. The CFO wants to roll it out company-wide.

**The question to the room:** did it work?

**Guide them to build the evaluation:** an untreated comparison group · the pre-period trend (was it already falling?) · difference-in-differences reasoning · and then the threats — **who got selected for the treatment**, regression to the mean, a sector-wide hiring freeze in the same window, Hawthorne effects, and the counterfactual nobody measured.

**Each group states one design that would have given a defensible answer if it had been set up before the money was spent.**

**Two reasons this is the strongest block in the series:** it needs no laptop, no data and no technical background from you — and it is the moment students realise the hard part of analytics is causal reasoning, not modelling. With 13 people all three groups get to pitch and the whole room hears three different designs.

**AT4 hook** *(confirm with Dr Lee)*: "how would you evaluate whether the intervention worked" is a section most groups will otherwise get wrong.

### What to prepare
Compa-ratio worksheet · the HK$3m scenario on one page · a raw-gap-vs-unexplained-gap slide with a worked example

## 8. Sunday 4 — Predicting Turnover
**"Who's leaving, and what do we do the day we know?"**

### The one big idea
> **A model tells you who might leave. It doesn't tell you what to do, who's allowed to know, or whether you should have looked.**

### Run sheet (120 min)

| Time | Block | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 0–10 | **The callback that closes the arc** | Put Sunday 1's workforce plan back on screen. *"Three weeks ago you planned 60 hires and you assumed 22% attrition. Today, 26% of them are leaving. Watch what that does to your plan."* Re-run one group's spreadsheet live with the higher number. |
| 10–35 | **Input A — turnover as an HR problem** | Taxonomy (voluntary/involuntary, functional/dysfunctional, **regretted/avoidable**); the full cost stack; FinTech drivers — vesting and bonus timing, funding cycles, tiny teams, external pull, young fast-promoted managers |
| 35–48 | **International comparison** | Section 3.6, question 4 — job-hopping norms. Fast and revealing. |
| 48–58 | **Break** | — |
| 58–72 | **Input B — turnover as a prediction problem** | What a model does, at concept level. The confusion matrix in plain English. False positive vs false negative **in dollars**. *When* vs *whether*. Anything deeper → Dr Lee. |
| 72–100 | **Activity — from score to action** | The 40-name list, plus the twist (below) |
| 100–115 | **Discussion — who gets to see this list?** | HR, the line manager, the person, nobody. Round the table — with 13 people you can get all of them on record. |
| 115–120 | **Close the series** | The staircase, climbed four times. What you hope they take into their careers. |

### The activity — from score to action
**Part 1 (15 min):** groups get 40 named fictional employees with a risk score, role, tenure, manager, and estimated replacement cost. They decide: where to draw the intervention line, what the intervention actually *is*, who is told, and what they'll measure in six months.

**Part 2 — the twist (13 min):** tell them the model's two strongest drivers are **"manager tenure under 12 months"** and **"no promotion in 30 months"**. Ask what that changes.

The answer you're steering to: it moves the problem from 40 individual retention payments to a **manager-capability and career-path problem** — a systemic fix, not 40 awkward conversations. That is the difference between predictive and prescriptive, and it lands better here than anywhere else in the series.

**No laptop needed.** Print the 40-row table.

**AT4 hook** *(confirm with Dr Lee)*: threshold-setting justified in cost terms is exactly what a strong project report does and a weak one skips.

### What to prepare
The 40-row printed table · cost-of-turnover worksheet · Sunday 1's spreadsheet, ready to re-run live

## 9. Staying in your lane

Dr Lee owns the technology content. That is a gift — it means you can say "not mine" without it being a weakness. Where the boundary sits:

| Yours | His |
|---|---|
| What the decision is, and who makes it | How the algorithm works |
| What the model is *for*, and what it must not be used for | How to build, tune or validate one |
| The four rungs as a way to frame a question | Model evaluation mechanics beyond reading a confusion matrix |
| Cost, fairness, law, governance, employee reaction | Software, code, statistics |
| What actually happens in a real HR meeting | Everything technical |

**Three honest ways to hand a question over:**
1. **Redirect to the decision** — *"I can't tell you how it weights that. What I can tell you is what happens when a manager gets that number and has to act on it, which is the part that goes wrong."*
2. **Hand it to Dr Lee** — *"That's a modelling question and he's better placed than me. Let's park it."*
3. **Say you don't know** — *"I don't know. My instinct is X, but don't quote me."*

**Never bluff a technical answer.** In a room of 13 you will be caught, and it costs you the authority on the HRM content, which is the part they actually can't get anywhere else.
